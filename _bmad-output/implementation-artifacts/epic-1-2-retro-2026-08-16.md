## 2. What went well

### 2.1 The zero-line-diff fence

Every Epic 2 story declared directories that had to show a literal zero-line diff and
verified it at completion. Story 2.4 held `backend/agent/**`, `llm/**`, `services/**`,
`domain/**`, `engine/**` and `migrations/**` all clean. Story 2.5 was the **first
intentional break** — and it had to be argued in the story, recorded in the ledger, and
the ledger entry named which future story inherits the new baseline (Story 2.6, which
then closed it). This is how a boundary survives nine stories of pressure without
quietly eroding.

### 2.2 The deferred-work ledger became a routing mechanism, not a graveyard

85 entries, 24 with an explicit owner or revisit trigger phrased as a *condition*
("the first story that persists per-run tool-call accounting", "the next story that
already has a legitimate reason to touch `gate_a_checks.py`") rather than a name or a
date. Ten entries are closed, each citing the commit and story that closed them.

Most notably, **three entries were corrected rather than deleted**:

- `:134` — a claim that `alembic.ini` was not checked in was falsified by Story 2.3's
  creation, which re-ran the command, found it was a working-directory mistake, and
  wrote *"No remediation work is owed and this item needs no owner"* while leaving the
  entry visible so the mistake would not be repeated.
- `:160` — Story 2.4 falsified its own assigned ownership of the `ScenarioCatalogueReader`
  AD-1 leak, explained precisely why tiers 1 and 2 would be *worse than leaving it*, and
  restated the owner as a condition.
- `:76`, `:81` — Story 2.8 corrected the premise of two Story 1.6 items rather than
  ticking them off.

A ledger that can say "this entry was wrong" is a ledger people will keep reading.

### 2.3 Story creation matured into a real discipline

Compare a Story 1.x note in `sprint-status.yaml` to a 2.x one. By Stories 2.7 and 2.9
the creation notes carry:

- **N decisions made at creation**, each recorded in the story
- **traps ranked quietest-first** — the failure modes most likely to pass review silently
- **honest gaps raised rather than papered over** — reductions stated as reductions
- **ledger routing by line number**, naming which entries the story closes, corrects, and
  must not touch
- **phased execution with a human checkpoint** and named abort conditions (2.7's Phase A
  had four)

None of this existed at Story 1.1. It is the reason a 1,332-line, 14-task story such as
2.7 landed at all.

### 2.4 The grounding mechanism (Minh's pick for most valuable output)

Story 2.7's fail-closed citation verification: every rendered number is
application-computed from an immutable version and carries an exact evidence locator, and
a claim that cannot be verified fails rather than rendering. Story 2.8 then made the
locator navigable and returnable. This is the artifact the milestone is *for*.

## 3. What did not go well

### 3.1 Tests that could not go red — the dominant quality pattern

Classifying every patch finding by whether it describes a guard that cannot fail
(vacuous, tautological, "cannot go red", never asserted / exercised / consumed, dead
code, proves nothing):

| | Epic 1 | Epic 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Findings of this class | **2** | **19** |

Representative examples:

- `The atomicity test is tautological` (2.3)
- `The concurrency test can never exercise uq_persisted_event_stream_sequence` (2.3)
- `PersistedEventV1 is dead code` — the write path inserted an untyped dict and the read
  path rehydrated one (2.3)
- `[HIGH] AC4's removal proof cannot go red` (2.6)
- `Task 9's self-redness proof does not exercise the conformance suite` (2.6)
- `Scope assertions are vacuous` (2.5)
- `Vacuous assertion in the "no file silently skipped" guard` (2.2)
- `timeout_seconds and remaining_budget are declared and never consumed` (2.5)

This is a systemic failure mode of invariant-shaped work, not carelessness. When the
deliverable is an invariant rather than a feature, the test is the *only* evidence the
invariant exists — and a test that cannot fail is indistinguishable from one that can
until someone reads it adversarially.

The team did converge on a remedy, late: Story 2.9's creation note demands
*"PROOF AN INJECTION CASE WAS OBSERVED TO FAIL with its structural assertion removed."*
Red-then-green as shipped evidence. That standard was learned in Epic 2, at 21 patches
a story.

### 3.2 The real cost centre: decision-grade findings surfaced at review

**Minh's own assessment, and the most important finding in this retrospective:** the
expensive part was never the patch volume. It was *resolving the decisions raised during
review, after the story had already been implemented.*

| Story | Decision-grade findings |
|---|---|
| **2.7** — Ground schedule claims in exact evidence | **9** |
| 2.6 — Governed capability module | 3 |
| 2.5 — Inspect scenario facts | 2 |
| 1.2 / 1.3 / 1.4 / 1.9 | 1 each |

Story 2.7 alone carries roughly half the decision load of twenty stories.

**Five of its nine trace to a single root cause.** The demand family/unit dimensional
model — `outbound` and `inbound` demand is measured in **volume**, `indirect` in
**headcount**; assignments carry worker identity but no family — is a real and stable
rule that **is written down nowhere in this repository as a first-class artifact.**

Every story that touched a metric re-derived it from adapter code, got it subtly wrong,
and it was adjudicated at review:

> - *"Demand `unit` is ignored, so `volume` rows are multiplied into 'minutes'"*
> - *"`shortfall_minutes` still mixes dimensions across the subtraction — the explicit
>   `family` argument was removed, the implicit one was not"*
> - *"A 'proven empty set' cannot be distinguished from '1541 rows were read and
>   discarded as the wrong dimension'"*
> - *"Three of the four metrics cannot return a supported claim on the only seeded
>   fixture"*
> - *"An application-side condition is reported as model fabrication, and a truthful
>   zero cannot render"*

The story-creation discipline (§2.3) records **decisions**. What was missing here was not
a decision — it was a **fact nobody had recorded**. Creation notes cannot anticipate a
rule that exists only in one person's head.

This tax will be paid again in Epic 3 unless the rule is written down: the ledger
schedules `shortfall_minutes` to return *"once CP-SAT supplies a per-worker rate"* and a
headcount metric *"when the solver makes staffing central"* — both Epic 3.

### 3.3 Declared-but-unsupplied seams

A recurring shape: a typed dimension is introduced and branched on, but no production
path ever supplies it, so the branch is unreachable and untestable.

- `remaining_budget` is a per-run snapshot that is never decremented, so
  `budget_exhausted` is unreachable through actual consumption (`:16`)
- `revoked_conversation_ids` is branched on by `compose_granted_capabilities`, but the
  only production construction site leaves it at its empty default (`:188`)
- `RunSource = Literal["double", "live"]` — the string `"live"` appears **nowhere else in
  `backend/evals/`**; no live runner, no gating, no budget (`:7`)
- `required_role` / `required_feature_policy` are self-declared strings with no
  registry-side allow-list (`:17`)

Each was a deliberate, recorded reduction. The concern is cumulative: several of these
are load-bearing for claims the project wants to make.

**The commercially significant one:** NFR28's "≥90% overall tool routing" currently
describes the **dataset**, not the model. Every golden case drives a deterministic double
whose turns are authored, and `ToolRoutingEvaluator` asserts exact equality against that
same authored expectation. Sound regression coverage — but reporting that pass rate as a
model-quality measure would overclaim, and it is the headline AI-engineering number.

### 3.4 Gate A operational friction

- **The readiness gate cannot be run twice in a row** (`:107`). `gate_a_readiness.main()`
  writes into `evidence/`, dirtying the tree, so the next `resolve_bindings()` raises
  `DirtyTreeError`. Every Epic 2 story paid what the notes call "the two-commit dance."
- **Evidence files have no expiry** (`:103`). Reproduced during the 1.11 review: taking
  real story-1.9 evidence, setting `git_commit` to a June commit predating PostgreSQL and
  `measurement_date` to `1999-01-01`, returns zero violations — fully bound. The exposure
  grows through Epics 3–5 as these files sit still while the code beneath them moves.

### 3.5 CI does not exist

`.github/workflows` is still absent. Every "normal CI" guarantee written across both
epics — AC1's zero-network default suite, the golden-case validation gate, the
`requires_git` evidence sweep — is enforced only by whoever remembers to run pytest
locally. Flagged in the Story 1.11 review *and* again in the Story 2.2 review. Still open.

## 4. Key insights

1. **Story creation records decisions; it cannot record facts nobody has written down.**
   The single most expensive pattern across both epics was a stable domain rule living
   only in the project lead's head, re-derived wrongly by every story that needed it, and
   adjudicated at the most expensive possible moment — after implementation.

2. **Invariant-shaped work fails by producing guards that cannot go red.** 19 findings of
   this class in Epic 2 versus 2 in Epic 1. The remedy the team converged on — demand a
   demonstrated red, with the structural assertion removed — should be the standard from
   the start of Epic 3, not discovered again.

3. **A ledger that can correct itself is worth keeping.** Three entries were falsified
   and rewritten rather than deleted. That is why the ledger is still trusted at 85
   entries.

4. **Conditional ownership beats assigned ownership.** "The first story that persists
   per-run tool-call accounting" survived being wrong about *which* story that was;
   "Story 2.4" did not.

5. **Epic 1's substrate is what made Epic 2 fast.** Nine harder stories in eight days on
   a governed, RLS-enforced, evidence-bound read surface. The investment paid.

## 7. Closing

Twenty stories delivered, Gate A green with an empty blocking list, and a grounded-answer
mechanism that is the point of the milestone. The suite grew from 212 backend tests to
851 while the architecture boundaries held.

The single most valuable output of this retrospective is not a metric — it is the
identification of where the real cost sat. Not the review volume, but the decisions that
review forced *after* implementation, and the fact that most of the expensive ones traced
to one unwritten domain rule. That rule is now the first action item.
